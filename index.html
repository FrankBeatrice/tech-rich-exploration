01 / THE LENSGREENWICH VILLAGE · NEW YORK

01

Culture lives
in the everyday.

A reflection on seeing, listening, and the cultural context of ordinary life.

Walking through Greenwich Village, I've come to believe that culture is defined not simply by a neighborhood's history or architecture, but by the everyday habits, traditions, and interactions of the people who occupy it. From the restaurants and street performers to the students and longtime residents, the Village reflects a combination of material and immaterial culture. Annie Dillard reminds us that seeing requires genuine attention, while Johnna S. Keller challenges us to consider how architecture influences who can participate in public life. Julian Treasure's emphasis on listening adds another dimension, suggesting that the sounds and conversations we often dismiss are equally important in understanding a community's identity.

What I found most interesting throughout this project was how much we overlook when we're focused on getting somewhere rather than experiencing where we are. Warren Berger's emphasis on curiosity encouraged me to question what I observed, while Kate Murphy reinforced the importance of listening to understand perspectives beyond our own. Sherry Turkle's concerns about technology replacing genuine conversation felt particularly relevant in a neighborhood where people are constantly surrounded by others, yet often disconnected. Paying closer attention to these interactions changed my understanding of culture. I began to recognize that culture isn't simply something we inherit or observe; it's something we actively create through our habits, values, and relationships with others.

FROM A FRAMEWORK TO A PLACE

Those questions about culture travel. The next six observations come from Recoleta and Balvanera, Buenos Aires—not Greenwich Village. Different streets, but the same impulse to look closer at what usually goes unnoticed.

02 / PLACES & THINGSBUENOS AIRES · ARGENTINA · 2022 / 2026 REFLECTIONS
A VISUAL FIELD JOURNAL

Six ways to
notice a city.

Original photographs and the complete descriptions from Places and Things Descriptions, October 6, 2026.

01 / 06Storefront humor

“Si no dejan propina, hay tabla.”

A handwritten-looking sign sits behind a shop window: “SI NO DEJAN PROPINA… HAY TABLA” translation: "DON'T LEAVE A TIP...THAT’S A PADDLING." Between these worn words is Jasper Beardly of Simpson’s infamy, an exhausted cartoon man staring forward with heavy eyes. The joke is aggressive without being serious, the type of humor that only works because everyone understands the exaggeration. The glass surrounding it is scratched and dusty, reflecting the street, the photographer, and the room behind it all at once. Even before entering a business, the neighborhood speaks to you.

Humor is posted in windows. Personality is part of the storefront. It is not polished or designed by a branding firm. Someone printed it, taped it up, and decided it belonged there.

02 / 06Streets and architecture

The Street

A piece of heaven exists, & it is in South America.

The streets connect. Solid pegs beneath your feet. Small stones and small square tiles construct the sidewalks while apartment buildings rise almost directly from them. Balconies hang above the street, crowded with plants, shutters, railings, and air-conditioning units. Wires stretch between buildings. Cars occupy nearly every available piece of curb. Trees interrupt the concrete.

At street level, businesses disappear beneath six or seven stories of climbing apartments, almost as though two cities have been stacked on top of each other. In 2022, I lived near the edge of Recoleta and Balvanera. On a map, the neighborhoods have names and boundaries. Walking through them, the distinction is less obvious. The streets are misnomers: peaceful yet chaotic, old and modern at the same time.

03 / 06Commerce as conversation

The Market

The market proves that bartering is not dead. Folding tables sit beneath temporary white tents, covered with spices, dried foods, bottles, pastries, and small containers whose contents are not immediately obvious. Nothing is hidden behind glass. Everything is pushed forward toward the customer. Buyers stop, inspect, question, and negotiate. Linguistic pugilists make deals, friendships, & perhaps enemies across the tables. There is something cinematic about it. A supermarket gives a price and waits for payment; here, commerce becomes conversation. Tone matters. Familiarity matters. Language matters. The person selling the food is standing only a few feet from the person deciding what it is worth.

04 / 06Art in everyday life

The Walls

Art consumes everything. On one white wall, a lanky neck giraffe stretches nearly to the roof, its body filled with dozens of smaller patterns. Beside it, to the left, sits the portrait of a girl underneath the black branches of a tree in pure white space. To the right, a cubist elephant, reminiscent of a Picasso painting. For all these paintings Albastor is the background of choice, in contrast to the dingy beige paint everywhere else. Neither image is hanging inside a gallery. They are painted directly onto an aging building, beside shutters, electrical boxes, barred windows, cracks, graffiti, and a residential door. The contrast gives the art its force. A pristine canvas would separate the image from daily life. Here, it’s both; the wall itself is part of the work. The building is allowed to age around it. Paint fades. Plaster cracks. Someone writes over something else. The neighborhood does not seem particularly concerned with preserving a clean division between art and ordinary life.

05 / 06Food and public space

The Food

The restaurants push food outward, toward the street. On the plate, meat is blackened at its edges and covered with onions and enormous green basil leaves. Behind it sit bread, vegetables, and sauces, slightly blurred by the camera. Argentine food can be heavy and uncomplicated in appearance: meat, fire, bread, and vegetables, however, the simplicity is deceptive. The smell arrives before the plate does. Smoke & caramelized meat mix with herbs while conversations continue around the table. Eating outdoors makes the restaurant another part of the street. Cars pass. People walk beside you. A bus may stop a few yards away. There is little separation between eating in the neighborhood and watching the neighborhood continue around you. Recopado

06 / 06The people behind a place

The People Give the Neighborhood Life

Trees form a canopy over another block, throwing broken patches of sunlight across cars, sidewalks, murals, and storefronts. An old low-rise building painted turquoise stands beside newer apartment towers. Posters and street art cover a neighboring wall while a modern bus occupies the curb. Nothing matches, yet nothing feels particularly out of place. That may be the defining feature of the neighborhood. Shops of immigrants, signs of yesteryear, older buildings, new construction, buses, motorcycles, cafés, and apartments all occupy the same few feet of street. The buildings provide the stage, but the people give the neighborhood life. They eat outside, negotiate at markets, hang signs in windows, paint walls, grow plants from balconies, and continue using spaces that might otherwise have disappeared.

Recoleta. Balvanera. Two names divided on a map. On foot, the streets simply connect.

THE CRAFTSMAN

Boris.

Jeweler · Monmouth County, New Jersey

Imagined setting: An independent jewelry shop in New Jersey. Approximate interview duration: 35 minutes.

FULL TRANSCRIPT

Craftsmanship, immigration, trust, memory, and what makes something valuable.

READ REFLECTION ↘

The shop is smaller than I expected. Glass cases occupy most of the floor. Behind the counter are watches, repair tools, and a workbench illuminated by a single lamp. Boris is examining a ring when I arrive. He puts it down, removes his glasses, and asks what I want to know.

Frankie

Tell me about the shop. What’s an ordinary day look like?

Boris

Ordinary? There is no ordinary. Somebody wants a ring resized. Somebody breaks a chain. Somebody comes in with something they’ve had for forty years and wants it repaired for twenty dollars.

Frankie

What do you usually do first?

Boris

Coffee. Then I see what people left me yesterday. Sometimes I think I will finish five repairs, but then somebody walks in, and the whole day changes.

Frankie

Where did you grow up?

Boris

In what you would call Ukraine today. My grandmother called it Poland. My grandfather called it Galicia. It depends on who is asking and when they are asking.

Frankie

So what do you call it?

Boris

Home. The politicians can decide what to call the place. I remember the streets, the buildings, the people. These things don’t change so easily.

Frankie

How did you become a jeweler?

Boris

I learned working with my hands. You watched somebody who knew what he was doing. At first, you weren’t trusted with anything important. You cleaned, polished, and watched. Eventually, somebody gave you something to fix.

Frankie

Do you remember the first thing you ruined?

Boris

(Laughs.) That’s a better question. Yes. A chain. Very thin. I overheated it. I thought if a little heat was good, a bit more heat would be better…It wasn’t.

Frankie

What did that teach you?

Boris

Patience. But you don’t learn patience because somebody tells you to be patient. You learn it because you make a mistake.

Frankie

Walk me through how you came to New Jersey.

Boris

It’s a long story. I didn’t come here because I wanted to become rich. I wanted an opportunity to work and make something for myself. When you’re young, you think starting over will be easier than it is.

Frankie

What was hardest about starting over?

Boris

People don’t know you. Back home, somebody knew your family, somebody knew your work. Here, you’re just another person asking somebody to trust you.

Frankie

How did you earn that trust?

Boris

One customer at a time. You do good work. They come back. They tell their friends. Sometimes it takes years.

Frankie

Is there one piece you’ve worked on that you still remember?

Boris

There was a woman who brought in an old ring. It wasn’t valuable, not in money. It belonged to her mother. She wanted it cleaned but was worried I would change it.

Frankie

Change it how?

Boris

Make it look new. I told her I could polish it, but I asked whether she wanted all the scratches gone. She didn’t. Those scratches were part of the ring she remembered.

Frankie

So the imperfections had value?

Boris

To her, yes. People think jewelry is about gold and diamonds. Sometimes it’s about a person who isn’t here anymore.

…I let the silence sit for several seconds…

Frankie

Do you ever think about the things you left behind?

Boris

Of course. Everybody does. But you cannot live your life looking backward. You can remember something and still move forward.

Frankie

Has the business changed?

Boris

Very much. People look at a picture online and expect you to make the same thing, immediately, cheaply. They don’t always understand the work.

Frankie

What do you think people misunderstand most about your profession?

Boris

That the price of something tells you what it is worth.

Frankie

What would you want someone younger to understand about the work?

Boris

Learn to do something properly. Not quickly. Properly. And learn when to leave something alone.

Frankie

Is there anything I should have asked you?

Boris

You asked me a lot about the past. You didn’t ask whether I still enjoy coming here.

Frankie

Do you?

Boris

Most mornings. (Laughs.) Ask me the question again tomorrow.

REFLECTION 01

What a ring
can hold.

I entered the interview expecting to hear about immigration and craftsmanship. Boris was a jeweler from a region whose political identity had changed repeatedly, & I assumed that history would dominate our conversation. Instead, what stayed with me was his understanding of value.

The most revealing moment came when he described an old ring a customer had brought into his shop. She wanted it cleaned, but not stripped of its imperfections. The scratches were part of what she remembered about her mother. I initially approached jewelry as something whose value could be measured by material, workmanship, or price. Boris made me consider the value of leaving something unchanged.

I also noticed my tendency to anticipate answers. When Boris discussed Galicia, I expected a discussion about national identity. His answer was much simpler: he remembered home through streets, buildings, and people rather than borders. My question had assumed the importance of political geography; his response revealed something more personal.

The strongest moments came from questions I had not planned. Asking about his first mistake led to a discussion of patience. Asking whether the ring’s imperfections had value opened an entirely different conversation. These moments reminded me that interviewing is not simply gathering answers to questions I have already written. It is recognizing when a response deserves greater attention. I can identify with his story, my Grandfather is from Poland, our family origins bear a similar story. People left because they needed better for their families, a lot accompanies that.

I also found silence difficult. After Boris described the ring, I wanted to move immediately to another question. Waiting gave the story space. Julian Treasure’s emphasis on listening became more practical than theoretical at that moment: I had to resist treating silence as something that needed to be filled.

What I would change is how quickly I moved from one subject to another. I wanted to cover his history, his work, his immigration, and his business. At times, those ambitions competed with the conversation itself.

Perhaps the most revealing exchange came at the end, when Boris pointed out that I had asked extensively about his past but never asked whether he still enjoyed his work. I had become so interested in understanding where he came from that I almost forgot to ask who he was today.

THE ENGINEER

The engineer.

Anonymous senior software engineer · Citadel

Imagined setting: A video interview with a software engineer in Illinois. Approximate interview duration: 35 minutes.

FULL TRANSCRIPT

Judgment, technology, uncertainty, the value of restraint, and who we are beyond our work.

READ REFLECTION ↘

The conversation begins with a brief agreement about anonymity. The engineer is comfortable discussing career experiences and general engineering principles, but not confidential systems, proprietary work, or internal company information.

Frankie

Tell me about an ordinary day. What does it actually look like?

Engineer

Probably less exciting than people imagine. Meetings, code reviews, planning. Sometimes debugging. A good portion of my day is understanding problems before anybody writes code.

Frankie

That’s interesting. I expected more programming.

Engineer

That’s what people think software engineers do all day. At a certain level, writing code is only part of the job.

Frankie

What separates somebody senior from someone who’s technically very good?

Engineer

Judgment. A junior engineer might solve the problem in front of them. A senior engineer has to understand whether that’s the right problem to solve.

Frankie

How did you learn that distinction?

Engineer

By getting things wrong. Early on, I thought the best engineers were the people who wrote the cleverest code. Later, I realized clever solutions can create complicated problems for everybody else.

Frankie

So what makes someone a great engineer?

Engineer

Somebody who makes complicated things understandable. Somebody other people trust. If you’re the only person who understands what you built, that isn’t necessarily a success.

Frankie

Does that become more important in financial technology?

Engineer

Reliability matters enormously in systems where mistakes can have consequences. But the principle isn’t exclusive to finance. You want systems that behave predictably, especially when something unexpected happens.

Frankie

Can you describe a moment when something went wrong, without getting into confidential details?

Engineer

I can give you a general example from my career. We were looking at an issue where the immediate instinct was to change the code. But first we needed to understand whether the software was wrong or whether our assumptions were wrong.

Frankie

What did you do first?

Engineer

Asked questions. Looked at what we knew. Tried to reproduce the behavior. You can make things worse by rushing to fix something you haven’t understood.

Frankie

How does that feel when people are waiting for an answer?

Engineer

Uncomfortable. But being uncomfortable isn’t an excuse to be careless.

Frankie

Has AI changed the way you work?

Engineer

It can accelerate some tasks. But generating code and understanding whether the code is correct are two different things.

Frankie

What don’t you trust it with?

Engineer

Anything I can’t adequately verify. The important question isn’t whether a tool can produce an answer. It’s whether you understand the answer well enough to take responsibility for it.

Frankie

What do people misunderstand about working as an engineer at a financial firm?

Engineer

They imagine the interesting part is the money or the technology. The interesting part, for me, is solving difficult problems with people who see things differently.

Frankie

Has your definition of success changed?

Engineer

Definitely. Early in your career, success can feel like proving you’re the smartest person in the room. Later, you realize it’s more valuable to work with people who make you realize what you don’t know.

Frankie

That’s almost the opposite of what I expected.

Engineer

Why?

Frankie

I suppose I associate highly technical environments with individual intelligence and competition.

Engineer

Those things exist in different forms. But intelligence alone isn’t enough. If nobody understands you, or nobody trusts your judgment, being technically brilliant only gets you so far.

Frankie

What’s the hardest thing about your work that people wouldn’t expect?

Engineer

Knowing when not to do something.

Frankie

What do you mean?

Engineer

Sometimes somebody proposes an improvement. It’s interesting. It might even work. But the costs and risks aren’t justified. Being experienced means you can explain why doing less might be better.

Frankie

What would you tell someone who wants to enter this field?

Engineer

Learn fundamentals. Build things. Read other people’s code. And learn to communicate.

Frankie

If you could go back and tell your younger self one thing, what would it be?

Engineer

You don’t have to prove you know everything. You have to be willing to find out what you don’t know.

Frankie

Is there something I haven’t asked that you think matters?

Engineer

You asked about work a lot. You didn’t ask what I do when I’m not working.

Frankie

Fair point. What do you do when you’re not working?

Engineer

Things that have absolutely nothing to do with computers. (Laughs.) Sometimes the best thing you can do for your work is stop thinking about it.

REFLECTION 02

Knowing when
not to act.

Sample reflection based on the imagined conversation.

I approached the second interview with a different set of assumptions. Unlike Boris, whose profession involved physical objects and traditional craftsmanship, the engineer worked in an environment I associated with speed, complexity, technical expertise, and competition. I expected to hear about programming languages, financial markets, and difficult technical problems. Instead, the conversation repeatedly returned to judgment, communication, and restraint.

The most surprising distinction was his explanation of what separates a senior engineer from someone less experienced. I assumed technical sophistication would be the deciding factor. He argued that knowing which problems deserve attention is often more valuable than simply knowing how to solve them.

His observation that sometimes the correct decision is not to change something reminded me of Boris’s approach to repairing jewelry. Both described expertise not only as the ability to act, but also as the judgment to recognize when action is unnecessary. One of my better follow-up questions came when I asked how it felt to resist making an immediate change while others were waiting for an answer. The question moved beyond technical procedure into the personal experience of responsibility. It revealed a tension between appearing decisive and making a sound decision.

I also became conscious of how easily an interview can turn into a discussion of my own interests. Because I am interested in technology and finance, I sometimes wanted to ask questions that would benefit my understanding of those industries. But the purpose of the assignment was not career research. It was to understand another person.

The anonymity of the conversation made attentive questioning particularly important. I needed to respect the boundary between professional experience and confidential information. That constraint ultimately encouraged more meaningful questions about learning, relationships, and decision-making.

Like Boris, the engineer ended by pointing out something I had overlooked. I had asked extensively about his professional identity without asking much about his life beyond it. Both interviews exposed the same weakness in my approach: I was interested in what people did before I was fully interested in who they were.

04 / CONNECTIONS

Look closer.
Ask better.
Listen longer.

The window sign, the market conversation, and the scratched ring each ask the same question: what makes something meaningful? A storefront speaks before anyone enters. A market turns commerce into conversation. An old object carries a memory that price cannot measure.

And the engineer introduces a final form of attention: judgment. Sometimes understanding requires asking another question, sometimes it requires leaving something unchanged. Across New York, Buenos Aires, and two imagined conversations, the connection is not geography. It is our willingness to see past the first impression.

Culture is not simply what we inherit or observe. It is something we participate in.
