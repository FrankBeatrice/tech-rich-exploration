THE JEWELER

BORIS
MONMOUTH COUNTY, NEW JERSEY
IMAGINED CONVERSATION

ON MEMORY & VALUE

The value of
a scratch.

I expected to hear about immigration and craftsmanship. What stayed with me was his understanding of value.

In the imagined jewelry shop, Boris describes a woman who brings in her mother's old ring. She wants it cleaned, but not made to look new. The scratches are part of what she remembers.

I'd thought of jewelry in terms of material, workmanship, and price. The ring forced another question: what happens when the marks we want to remove are the very things that give an object meaning?

“Sometimes it’s about a person who isn’t here anymore.”

BORIS — FICTIONAL DIALOGUE

When I asked where he grew up, I expected a discussion of changing borders—Poland, Ukraine, Galicia. Instead, he remembered home through streets, buildings, and people. I had decided where the conversation should go before he'd answered.

The better questions arrived unexpectedly: What was the first thing you ruined? Did the imperfections have value? And, just as importantly, could I let a silence sit without rushing to fill it?

WHAT I OVERLOOKED

Value isn't always something you can measure, polish, or replace.

THE ENGINEER

ANONYMOUS SENIOR ENGINEER
VIDEO CONVERSATION
IMAGINED CONVERSATION

ON JUDGMENT & RESTRAINT

Before the first
line of code.

I associated engineering with speed, complexity, and technical skill. The imagined conversation kept returning to judgment.

What surprised me was how much of the work happened before anybody wrote code. The engineer described a senior person's responsibility not simply as solving problems, but as deciding whether the right problem had been identified in the first place.

A change might be clever. It might even work. But that doesn't mean its cost and risk are justified. Acting quickly can look like confidence, even when the more responsible choice is to wait.

“You can make things worse by rushing to fix something you haven’t understood.”

THE ENGINEER — FICTIONAL DIALOGUE

I also noticed how easily my own interests in finance and technology shaped the questions I asked. I wanted to understand the industry. But the point was to understand a person. Those are not the same task.

His final observation brought that distinction home: I'd asked about work, but barely anything about life beyond it.

WHAT I OVERLOOKED

Being able to solve a problem does not mean you should.

THE CONNECTION

TWO TRADES. ONE IDEA.

The discipline
of doing less.

One worked with physical objects. The other, with systems. Both described expertise as knowing what deserves to be left alone.

01 / BORIS

Preserve the story.

Not every scratch needs removing. A repair can take something away.

02 / THE ENGINEER

Question the change.

Not every improvement needs implementing. A solution can create a new problem.

The common lesson wasn't about jewelry or technology. It was about patience, trust, and understanding the consequences of action.

THE REFLECTION

NOTES ON LISTENING
WHAT THE EXERCISE REVEALED

THE QUESTION BEHIND THE QUESTION

I was listening
for the wrong thing.

Both imagined conversations ended by exposing the same blind spot. Boris noticed I hadn't asked if he still enjoyed his work. The engineer noticed I hadn't asked what he did when he wasn't working.

I was interested in what people did before I was fully interested in who they were.

That is what I would carry into an actual interview: fewer assumptions about where an answer should lead, more willingness to follow it somewhere unexpected. Ask a better question. Listen to the answer. Let the silence do some of the work.

AN ENDING, NOT AN ANSWER

Sometimes understanding begins when we stop trying to get somewhere.
